# Stock Prediction Delivers Small Edges, Large Errors

For large US stocks over a 12-month horizon, no traditional method predicts price with useful precision, but a few slow, fundamentals-based cross-sectional signals (profitability and quality, intermediate-term momentum, value paired with profitability, and net share issuance) tilt the odds modestly and durably. The best published out-of-sample forecasts explain only about **2.5% (a simple three-characteristic linear model) to 4.9% (the best machine-learning model) of the annual return variance of the 1,000 largest US stocks**. That implies a per-stock directional hit rate of roughly 55–57%, because about 60% of a typical large stock's return variance is firm-specific news that no model sees coming. Investment banks build 12-month price targets mostly as a forward P/E multiple times the analyst's own EPS forecast. Those targets are met at the horizon only **about 38% of the time**, imply returns **about 15 points higher** than what follows, and miss by **45% on average**. The level of a Buy/Hold/Sell rating carries no reliable net-of-cost value among the few hundred largest firms. Commercial rating products advertise outperformance in the thousands of percent, but almost all of it is self-reported, gross of costs, equal-weighted and often backtested. The one long live, net-of-fee record found, the IBD 50 ETF, returned **1.88% a year against 13.29% for the S&P 500** from 2015 to 2024. Smart-beta indexes that beat the market by about 3% a year on paper trail it once live. For the proposed S&P 500 app, the evidence argues for redefining the weakest pillars (historical growth carries no premium, and standalone leverage and accrual signals are weak or decayed), scoring within sectors with weights fixed in advance, publishing wide bear/base/bull ranges that assume valuation gaps close over about three years rather than one, defining ratings against a benchmark with a deliberately balanced distribution, and grading only live, time-stamped calls against base rates for several years before claiming skill.

## Even the best models explain under 5% of a large stock's annual return

The academic starting point is that prices are hard to predict but not impossible to predict. Variance-ratio tests on weekly index returns from 1962 to 1985 rejected the random walk, but the rejections came largely from small stocks ([Lo & MacKinlay](https://web.mit.edu/Alo/www/Papers/lo-mackinlay-88.html)). Recent work finds autocorrelations at daily to monthly horizons modest, and slightly negative, at the industry and firm level ([Campbell, Lettau, Malkiel & Xu 2023](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29916/w29916.pdf)). Two findings reshaped the field. Shiller showed that stock prices swing far more than later dividends can justify ([Shiller 1981](https://www.nber.org/papers/w0456)), and later work showed that expected returns themselves drift slowly over time. Cochrane's presidential address quantifies the drift. Regressing CRSP value-weighted excess returns on the dividend yield over 1947–2009 gives an **R² of 9% at one year and 28% at five years**, measured in sample ([Cochrane 2011](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16972/w16972.pdf)). The resulting consensus holds that day-to-month moves in liquid stocks are nearly unpredictable while multi-year expected returns vary with valuations. That consensus carries a caveat that matters for any rating product. Efficiency can only be tested jointly with a model of risk, so a signal that "works" may be collecting a risk premium rather than finding mispricing. Buy-rated stocks may simply be riskier ones.

Out of sample, forecasting the market as a whole mostly collapses. Welch and Goyal found that none of the classic predictors (dividend and earnings yields, book-to-market, interest rates, term and default spreads, inflation, equity issuance) would have helped a real-time investor beat the historical-mean equity premium ([SFI summary of Welch & Goyal](https://sfi.ch/en/publications/a-comprehensive-2022-look-at-the-empirical-performance-of-equity-premium-prediction)). Campbell and Thompson's monthly out-of-sample R² values show how small the numbers are. They report **−0.33% for the dividend-price ratio, +0.26% for the earnings-price ratio, +0.10% for a CAPE-style smoothed earnings yield and −1.33% for book-to-market**, and only slight improvement when coefficients are forced to take the theoretically correct sign ([Campbell & Thompson, NBER w11468](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11468/w11468.pdf)). Their counterpoint is that a tiny R² still matters to a portfolio. A 0.25% monthly R² from the earnings yield would lift a mean-variance investor's return by roughly 1% a year at moderate risk aversion. Goyal, Welch and Zafirov's 2024 update re-tested 29 newer predictors plus the 17 originals through 2021. More than a third of the new ones were no longer significant even in sample, about half of the rest performed poorly out of sample, and only a handful still worked ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v37y2024i11p3490-3557..html); [Welch](https://ivo-welch.org/home/card-eqpredict.html)). For an app publishing 12-month price ranges, the practical lesson is to assume a neutral, unconditional market return and to grade calls against benchmarks. Grading against a benchmark strips out the market-timing problem, so any skill has to come from ranking stocks against each other.

Ranking stocks against each other does better, but not by much. The most-cited out-of-sample benchmark tests 94 firm characteristics on all US stocks over 1987–2016 ([Gu, Kelly & Xiu, NBER w25398](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf)). Its machine-learning rows appear below only because the paper compares them directly with linear models; a separate report covers those methods.

| Model (out-of-sample R², %) | Monthly, all stocks | Monthly, top 1,000 | Annual, all stocks | Annual, top 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OLS on size, book-to-market, momentum | 0.16 | 0.31 | 2.50 | **2.48** |
| OLS on all 920 features | −3.46 | **−11.28** | −34.86 | **−54.86** |
| Random forest | 0.33 | 0.63 | 3.28 | 4.80 |
| Neural net (3 layers) | 0.40 | **0.70** | 3.40 | 4.73 |
| Neural net (4 layers) | 0.39 | 0.67 | 3.60 | **4.91** |

Three features of this table shape an S&P 500 product. First, **annual R² is roughly ten times monthly R²**, and at the annual horizon industry membership and risk measures gain importance relative to price trends. That favors a 12-month, fundamentals-driven design. Second, **putting every predictor into a linear regression is catastrophic among large stocks**, at −11.28% monthly and −54.86% annually. A filings-based composite must therefore be parsimonious. The simple three-characteristic model reaches about 2.5% annually for the top 1,000 stocks, which is a realistic ceiling for a transparent linear score. Third, the authors measure R² against a forecast of zero. They note that benchmarking against the noisy historical mean would inflate R² by about three points, so the app's track record should use zero, or the sector return, as its null.

These R² figures can be converted into hit rates. Under a bivariate-normal approximation, the probability that forecast and outcome share a sign is 0.5 + arcsin(√R²)/π; this is my calculation applied to the published R² figures. An annual R² of 2.5% implies getting direction right about **55% of the time**. The best large-cap figure, 4.9%, implies about **57%**, and the best monthly large-cap figure, 0.7%, implies 52.7%. Individual 12-month calls will therefore be wrong almost half the time even if the model is as good as anything published. Skill shows up only in aggregates, such as the return spread between a Buy list and a Sell list.

The reason is how a large stock's return variance splits. For a value-weighted (large-cap-tilted) US stock in data through 2021, about **30% of return variance comes from the market and about 60% is firm-specific**, with industry making up the rest. The firm-specific share was 60–80% during 1962–1997, fell below half during the financial crisis, and is about 80% for the typical equal-weighted, smaller stock ([CLMX 2023](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29916/w29916.pdf)). A call graded against SPY is therefore a bet on sector and stock outcomes. A call graded against a sector ETF removes market and some industry variance, and what remains is mostly firm-specific news, the least predictable component. GICS sectors are coarser than the industries in that study, so a sector benchmark probably removes less variance than these shares suggest.

Professional results confirm how hard the problem is. In 2025, **78.8% of US large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500**, and the figure was **89.0% over five years and 92.9% over twenty**. On a risk-adjusted basis 98.6% trailed over fifteen years, and only half the large-cap funds that existed fifteen years earlier survived to be measured ([SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2025](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf)). Those figures are net of fees. Institutional accounts measured gross of fees, which are closer to the app's cost-free "paper" returns, did somewhat better: 67% trailed in 2025 ([SPIVA Institutional](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/institutional-spiva-scorecard/)). Bootstrap tests find few funds with enough benchmark-adjusted return to cover their costs ([Fama & French 2010](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v65y2010i5p1915-1947.html)). Single-stock returns are also highly skewed. About **4% of listed companies created all net US stock-market wealth** from 1926 to 2016, and 57.4% of stocks had lifetime returns below one-month Treasury bills ([Bessembinder, via ASU](https://news.wpcarey.asu.edu/20180917-do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)). Because of that skew, 50% is the wrong yardstick for Buy-call accuracy. The right yardstick is the share of S&P 500 members that beat the benchmark over the same window.

## Profitability and momentum survive in large caps; historical growth never paid

The cross-sectional literature reduces to a short list of characteristics surrounded by a crowded "factor zoo." Harvey, Liu and Zhu catalogued **316 published factors**, argued that new ones should clear a t-statistic of 3.0 rather than 2.0, and concluded that most claimed findings are likely false ([NBER w20592](https://nber.org/papers/w20592)). Three large replication projects look contradictory but answer different questions. Chen and Zimmermann asked whether published results reproduce. They found that **98% of 161 clearly significant predictors** do, with a slope of 0.90 between reproduced and original t-statistics ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/open-source-cross-sectional-asset-pricing.htm)). Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen tested 153 factors in 93 countries and found roughly 85% replicate and cluster into 13 themes ([NBER w28432](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28432)). Hou, Xue and Zhang asked a different question: what survives once microcaps are neutralized? Microcaps are about 60% of stocks but only 3% of market value. With NYSE breakpoints and value-weighting, **65% of 452 anomalies fail a conventional significance test and 82% fail a multiple-testing hurdle**. Value, momentum, investment and profitability survive best ([Hou, Xue & Zhang](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v33y2020i5p2019-2133..html)).

Decay after publication then shrinks what survives. Across 97 predictors, McLean and Pontiff found average monthly long-short returns of 0.582% in sample, 0.402% out of sample but before publication, and **0.264% after publication**. That is a 26% drop attributable at most to data mining and a 58% drop overall ([McLean & Pontiff](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf)). Decay was larger for signals built only from price and volume data, and post-publication returns were lower in portfolios concentrated in liquid, low-idiosyncratic-risk stocks, which describes the S&P 500. Mining 29,000 accounting ratios for t > 2 produces predictors that decay about as much as peer-reviewed ones, keeping roughly half their predictability after the sample ends. Predictors with risk-based explanations decayed more, not less ([Chen, Lopez-Lira & Zimmermann](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.10317v5)). A workable rule is to start from large-cap, value-weighted evidence and halve it.

Trading costs filter the list further. Most anomalies with one-sided monthly turnover below 50% keep significant net spreads, and few higher-turnover strategies do. A buy/hold spread, meaning a stricter rule for entering a position than for keeping it, is the single most effective cost mitigation. Size, value and profitability have the greatest capacity ([Novy-Marx & Velikov](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20721)). After effective spreads and post-publication decay, the **average anomaly's expected return is about 4 basis points a month**. The strongest reach about 10, and combinations of anomalies net about 20, before any price impact ([Chen & Velikov](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/zeroing-in-on-the-expected-returns-of-anomalies.htm)). A ratings product does not trade, but these numbers still matter as a filter on the evidence. Premia that exist only gross of costs, or only in microcaps, should not drive calls on S&P 500 names.

### The app's six pillars have very unequal support

**Profitability** has the strongest and most consistent case. Gross profits-to-assets predicts returns about as well as book-to-market. The most profitable firms beat the least profitable by 0.31% a month over 1962–2010, with a Fama-French three-factor alpha of 0.52% a month ([Novy-Marx, NBER w15940](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15940); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/?p=59260)). In what Robeco's Blitz called a lost decade, the four non-market Fama-French factors averaged −0.28% a year over 2010–2019 against +3.95% over 1963–2009, and only profitability stayed clearly positive ([Blitz, Robeco](https://robeco.com/files/docm/docu-robeco-factor-performance-2010-2019-a-lost-decade.pdf)). A secondary aggregator of Ken French's data, worth verifying before publication, puts the profitability factor at about +2.7% a year both before and after 2007. It also shows a 22.5% fall from an October 2023 peak, so even this factor has long flat stretches ([finobservatory](https://finobservatory.org/factors)). Quality-minus-junk, which combines profitability, growth, safety and payout, extends the same idea ([Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/60211462/lasse_heje_pedersen_et_al_quality_minus_junk_publishersversion.pdf)).

**Momentum** is strong historically and works in big stocks. Past 3–12-month winners beat losers by roughly 1% a month in the original US sample ([Jegadeesh & Titman](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v48y1993i1p65-91.html)). The effect shows no reliable relation with firm size ([Israel & Moskowitz, AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/The-Role-of-Shorting-Firm-Size-and-Time-on-Market-Anomalies)), and it was strong among big stocks through 2005 ([Fama & French, "Dissecting Anomalies"](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/fama_french_dissecting_anomalies_JF.pdf)). Its weakness is crashes. Over 1927–2013 a winner-minus-loser portfolio had a Sharpe ratio of 0.71 against 0.40 for the market. But past losers rose **232% in July–August 1932 and 163% in March–May 2009**, while winners gained 8% in the 2009 episode. These crashes are partly forecastable: they come in rebounds after market declines, when volatility is high, and scaling exposure by forecast mean and variance roughly doubles momentum's Sharpe ratio ([Daniel & Moskowitz](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439)). Gross momentum has earned only about 0.7% a year since 2007 ([finobservatory](https://finobservatory.org/factors)). Momentum also needs price data, not SEC filings, so it cannot come from filings alone.

**Value** has a long-run premium that has nearly vanished among the largest stocks. Big value stocks beat the market by 0.36% a month in 1963–1991 but only **0.05% a month in 1991–2019** ([Fama & French, via Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/value-stock-premium-shrinking)). The value premium weakens with firm size ([Israel & Moskowitz](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/The-Role-of-Shorting-Firm-Size-and-Time-on-Market-Anomalies)). The long-short HML factor fell about 58% from December 2006 to September 2020 and in May 2026 was still about a third below its peak ([finobservatory](https://finobservatory.org/factors)). Part of the problem is measurement. Book value ignores intangible assets, and capitalizing intangibles would have improved HML by 2.2 percentage points a year since 2008 ([Arnott, Harvey, Kalesnik & Linnainmaa, Research Affiliates](https://www.researchaffiliates.com/insights/journal-papers/reports-of-values-death-may-be-greatly-exaggerated)). Controlling for profitability also greatly improves value strategies ([Novy-Marx](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15940)).

**Growth**, measured as historical asset, sales or earnings growth, has no premium. Low-asset-growth firms beat high-asset-growth firms by 8.4% a year value-weighted in the original study ([Cooper, Gulen & Schill, via CXO](https://cxoadvisory.com/fundamental-valuation/asset-growth-cross-section-stock-returns)). Yet there is no asset-growth anomaly among the big stocks that make up more than 90% of market value ([Fama & French 2008](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/fama_french_dissecting_anomalies_JF.pdf)). Among large caps, then, historical growth neither helps nor reliably hurts. "Good growth" survives only in other forms: profitability, or the forecast investment growth used in the q5 model ([Hou, Mo, Xue & Zhang, via IBKR](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/campus/ibkr-quant-news/building-a-better-q-factor-asset-pricing-model/)).

**Balance-sheet strength** has little standalone support. Piotroski's nine-signal F-score, which includes leverage, liquidity and issuance, raised returns within high book-to-market stocks by at least 7.5% a year. Its long-short spread of about 23% a year over 1976–1996 came heavily from small caps ([Piotroski 2000](https://test2.ivey.uwo.ca/media/3775523/value_investing_the_use_of_historical_financial_statement_information.pdf)). Highly distressed stocks do earn low returns, but the pattern is concentrated where arbitrage frictions are high, which excludes most S&P 500 names ([Campbell, Hilscher & Szilagyi](https://econbiz.de/Record/in-search-of-distress-risk-campbell-john/10010295772)).

**Earnings quality** has been published and has decayed. Hedge returns to Sloan's accruals strategy decayed until they were no longer reliably positive, a decline the authors partly attribute to hedge-fund capital trading the strategy ([Green, Hand & Soliman](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v57y2011i5p797-816.html)). One Columbia study disputes the decline ([Columbia](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/persistence-accruals-anomaly)), and accruals did work among big stocks through 2005 ([Fama & French 2008](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/fama_french_dissecting_anomalies_JF.pdf)).

| Pillar | Large-cap evidence | Recent record | Evidence-based role in the app |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profitability / quality | Strong; best-replicating family | Only Fama-French factor clearly positive in 2010–2019 | Highest weight |
| Momentum | Works across sizes; crash-prone | About +0.7%/yr gross since 2007 | High weight, scaled down after market falls in high volatility; needs price data |
| Valuation | Big-stock premium ~0.05%/mo since 1991 | −58% HML drawdown 2006–2020 | Multi-metric, sector-relative, paired with profitability |
| Growth (historical) | No premium; asset-growth effect absent in big stocks | Investment factor about −0.3%/yr since 2007 | Near-zero weight, or reframe as capital discipline (low issuance and investment) |
| Balance sheet | No standalone priced factor; distress effect strongest in illiquid stocks | Not verified for large caps after 2010 | Safety screen or quality component |
| Earnings quality (accruals) | Strong through 2005; later decayed | Disputed | Low weight; cash-based profitability and red-flag overrides |

The investment-factor and post-2007 figures in this table come from the same secondary aggregator ([finobservatory](https://finobservatory.org/factors)) and should be checked against the French library before they appear in product copy.

### The last decade was a regime-driven, concentrated test

Over the 10 years to September 2026, the MSCI USA Enhanced Value index returned **13.92% a year against 14.75% for its parent, with a Sharpe ratio of 0.64 against 0.81**. Calendar years swung widely, from −1.15% against +20.73% in 2020 to +31.85% against +17.31% in 2025 ([MSCI Enhanced Value factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/255599/msci-usa-enhanced-value-index-usd-net.pdf)). The MSCI USA Momentum index returned 15.84% a year with a Sharpe ratio of 0.77 and 113% annual turnover ([MSCI Momentum factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/255599/msci-usa-momentum-index-usd-net.pdf)). Neither beat the cap-weighted market on a risk-adjusted basis, and both are now heavily concentrated. **Micron is 23.89% of the value index, and information technology is 43.61% of the value index and 52.46% of the momentum index.** S&P Dow Jones Indices attributed most of value's 2024 underperformance to the Magnificent Seven ([S&P DJI](https://spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/performance-reports/dashboard-sp-500-factor-2024-12.pdf)). A factor composite that is not scored within sectors and capped can turn into a hidden sector bet.

### How to combine signals

The combination evidence favors simple, integrated composites. AQR found that scoring each stock on all factors at once ("integrating") beats blending single-factor portfolios ("mixing") for long-only investors ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/White-Papers/Long-Only-Style-Investing)). Research Affiliates counters that integration's advantage appears mainly in concentrated portfolios and raises turnover ([Research Affiliates](https://www.researchaffiliates.com/documents/2019-webinars/Multi-Factor%20Design-Mixing-vs-Integrating.pdf)). Timing factors on value spreads is harder than it looks. In AQR's test, timing the betting-against-beta factor cut its Sharpe ratio from 0.53 to 0.40 ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Contrarian-Factor-Timing-is-Deceptively-Difficult)). Characteristics measured relative to industry peers are better proxies for expected returns than raw characteristics ([Asness, Porter & Stevens](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Working-Paper/Predicting-Stock-Returns-Using-IndustryRelative-Firm-Characteristics)). Neutralizing sectors on average helps long-short investors and does not help long-only investors ([Ehsani, Harvey & Li](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P165_Is_sector_neutrality.pdf)). A sector-relative grade is in effect a within-sector long-short test, so sector-relative scoring fits the app's grading.

### Signals beyond the six: what survives in free SEC data

Several other traditional signals were tested for large caps and free data. Classic technical analysis fails. Across 7,846 trading rules on the Dow from 1897 to 2011, the best rules could not be identified in advance, and since 1962 most rules lose money even before trading costs ([Bajgrowicz & Scaillet](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/eee/jfinec/v106y2012i3p473-491.html)). Post-earnings-announcement drift, worth about 4.2% over 60 days in 1974–1985 ([Bernard & Thomas, via Event Study Tools](https://www.eventstudytools.com/earnings-announcements)), has been **absent for stocks above the NYSE 20th size percentile since 2006**. Over the same period the two-day price response to an earnings surprise rose from 20 to 120 basis points, meaning prices now absorb surprises almost immediately ([Martineau 2022](https://cfr.ivo-welch.org/published/papers/martineau2021rest.pdf)). A 2025 replication over 2001–2024 finds the drift factor insignificant once microcaps are excluded, with a t-statistic of 1.43 ([UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/is-post-earnings-announcement-drift-a-thing-again/)). Stock-level short interest predicts underperformance of 215 basis points a month equal-weighted, but only an insignificant 39 basis points value-weighted ([Asquith, Pathak & Ritter](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/357555)).

Three signals do survive and can be built from free EDGAR data. **Net share issuance** predicts returns more strongly than book-to-market, size or momentum since 1970, over horizons from one month to three years ([Pontiff & Woodgate](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v63y2008i2p921-945.html)). **Opportunistic insider purchases**, meaning those that do not follow a routine calendar pattern, earn 82 basis points a month value-weighted, while routine trades earn about nothing ([Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16454)). Insider sales carry no predictive information ([Lakonishok & Lee](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v14y2001i1p79-111.html)), and insider-purchase returns do not differ significantly between small and large firms ([Jeng, Metrick & Zeckhauser](https://www.nber.org/papers/w6913)). **Year-over-year changes in 10-K and 10-Q language** ("Lazy Prices") earned up to 188 basis points a month of alpha over 1995–2014, with no announcement-day reaction ([Cohen, Malloy & Nguyen](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25084)). That figure is a best-case, gross specification that has not been verified for large caps after 2014. Accounting events fit better as red flags than as ranked scores. Restatements cost about 9% in a two-day window but are priced at once ([Palmrose, Richardson & Scholz](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/412647)). Going-concern opinions are followed by about −14% drift over a year, but the opportunity is limited after costs and the drift is contested ([Kausar, Taffler & Tan](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/the-going-concern-market-anomaly/)).

## Sell-side targets are met at the horizon only 38% of the time and run 15 points optimistic

Investment banks set targets mainly with earnings multiples. In 1,126 reports by Institutional Investor All-America analysts from 1997–1999, **99.1% cited an earnings multiple and only 12.8% used any discounted cash flow model** ([Asquith, Mikhail & Au, NBER w9246](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9246.pdf)). In a survey of 365 analysts, 61% said they always use P/E or PEG and 60% always use a cash-flow model, against about 5% for residual-income models ([Brown, Call, Clement & Sharp](https://shareholderforum.com/ACCESS/Library/20130300_Brown-Call-Clement-Sharp.pdf)). The choice of method did not change target accuracy or the market's reaction. The only consistent result was that analysts using an idiosyncratic, proprietary method hit their targets less often. Because a target is a multiple applied to the analyst's own forward EPS, errors in the multiple and in the earnings forecast compound.

Academic tests show what each method can achieve at its best. Across US firms in 1982–1999, multiples built on forward earnings forecasts priced **about half of firms within 15% of their actual price**. Accuracy ranked forward earnings first, then historical earnings, then cash flow and book value, with sales last. The interquartile range of pricing errors was 0.317 for two-year-ahead EPS against 0.738 for sales ([Liu, Nissim & Thomas 2002](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/318/Equity_Valuation_Using_Multiples.pdf)). The same study found that industry harmonic means beat means and medians, that using all firms as comparables rather than industry peers degraded accuracy substantially, and that enterprise-value sales multiples performed worse than equity-value ones. Widening the sample beyond the profitable, positive-cash-flow firms in the main test raised the interquartile ranges for earnings multiples to 0.40–0.45. That wider range is the realistic accuracy for an engine using trailing SEC data. Discounted cash flow does about as well when fed management's own forecasts. In 51 leveraged transactions, 49–65% of DCF valuations landed within 15% of the deal price, against 37% for comparable-company EBITDA multiples ([Kaplan & Ruback](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4724.pdf)). When identical forecasts feed different present-value models, residual-income and accrual-earnings models produce smaller errors than DCF or dividend models, mainly because they lean less on the terminal value ([Penman & Sougiannis](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/coacre/v15y1998i3p343-383.html); [Francis, Olsson & Oswald](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2001/02/comparing-the-accuracy-and-explainability-of-dividend-free-cash-flow-and-abnormal-earnings-eq)). The terminal value dominates DCF results. For young firms it can be 90% to more than 100% of value. Damodaran's consistent closure caps stable growth at or below the risk-free rate and ties reinvestment to growth through return on capital. He calls exit multiples taken from comparables a dangerous mix of relative and intrinsic valuation ([Damodaran, ch. 12](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/valn2ed/ch12.pdf); [Damodaran, young firms](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/papers/younggrowth.pdf)). Discount rates matter as much. Moving the assumed market risk premium from 6% to 9% swung Kaplan and Ruback's median valuation errors by about 20 percentage points.

A gap between model value and price does predict returns, but mostly over two to three years. In US data for 1987–2015, the top-minus-bottom quintile spread on residual-income value-to-price was **5.3% at 12 months, 13.7% at 24 months and 27.8% at 36 months** (raw, gross). Book-to-market produced 4.1% at 12 months on the same basis ([Haboub, Kartsaklas & Sarafidis 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00206)). The same review notes that fewer than 30% of extreme value-to-price stocks converge to fundamental value within 36 months. A separate study finds that value-to-price adds nothing beyond its components, especially analyst earnings forecasts ([Xu 2007](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/14757700710835069/full/html)), so a trailing-only version will behave much like a blend of book-to-market and earnings yield. Morningstar likewise expects prices to converge on its fair values within about three years ([Morningstar methodology](https://www.morningstar.com/content/dam/marketing/shared/research/methodology/705988Morningstar_Equity_Research_Methodology.pdf)). A 12-month rating captures only the first part of this effect.

Measured against outcomes, 12-month targets are optimistic and imprecise. For US targets in 2000–2009, **about 38% were met at the end of 12 months and about 64% at some point during the year**. Target-implied returns exceeded realized returns by 15 percentage points on average, and absolute errors averaged 45% ([Bradshaw, Brown & Huang 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v18y2013i4d10.1007_s11142-012-9216-5.html)). Differences in target accuracy between analysts were statistically significant but economically weak, and the market did not react more to revisions from historically accurate analysts. Accuracy falls as promised upside rises. Among the 1997–1999 All-America targets, those implying 0–10% upside were touched within a year 74% of the time, while those implying more than 70% upside were touched less than a quarter of the time ([Asquith, Mikhail & Au](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9246.pdf)). The average implied upside was 32.9%, and even downgrades to Hold implied +5.5%.

Ratings help less than their prominence suggests. Over 1985–1996, the most favorably rated stocks earned gross abnormal returns of about 4% a year. But daily rebalancing pushed turnover above 400% a year, net returns were not reliably above zero, and **among the few hundred largest firms there was no reliable difference between top- and bottom-rated stocks** ([Barber, Lehavy, McNichols & Trueman 2001](https://www.econ.sdu.edu.cn/__local/7/26/CB/D35D313131640B8A5AA5DC9E205_D1BDD5A4_2B8A0.pdf?e=.pdf)). Rating changes moved prices more, especially Sells, which drifted −9.1% over six months in 1989–1991 ([Womack](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v51y1996i1p137-67.html)). For 2003–2010, however, average drift after a revision is no longer distinguishable from zero ([Altınkılıç, Hansen & Ye](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v119y2016i2p371-398.html)). Two findings point toward what makes a rating informative. Upgrades from brokers that issue the fewest Buys outperform upgrades from brokers that issue the most ([Barber et al. 2006](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/buys-holds-sells-distribution-investment-banks-stock-ratings)). Independent research firms' Buy calls beat investment-bank Buy calls by about 8 percentage points annualized in 1996–2003 ([Barber, Lehavy & Trueman 2007](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v85y2007i2p490-517.html)). After MiFID II unbundled research payments in Europe, coverage shrank but individual forecasts became more accurate ([Fang, Hope, Huang & Moldovan](https://biopen.bi.no/bi-xmlui/handle/11250/2992577)).

The biases in sell-side output are structural. In June 2025, the 12,319 ratings on S&P 500 stocks were **56.4% Buy, 38.7% Hold and 4.9% Sell** ([FactSet](https://advantage.factset.com/hubfs/Website/Resources%20Section/Research%20Desk/Earnings%20Insight/EarningsInsight_062725C.pdf)). Targets appeared in over 90% of Buy reports but in only 11.1% of Hold reiterations, so targets tend to go missing exactly when news is bad ([Asquith, Mikhail & Au](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9246.pdf)). The least-favored stocks skew toward small firms, which fits analysts' reluctance to issue Sells on large firms that could become banking clients ([Barber et al. 2001](https://www.econ.sdu.edu.cn/__local/7/26/CB/D35D313131640B8A5AA5DC9E205_D1BDD5A4_2B8A0.pdf?e=.pdf)). Analysts rank forecast accuracy and the profitability of their recommendations among the least important drivers of their pay. 82.7% call broker votes very important to their careers, and industry knowledge is what clients value most ([Brown et al.](https://shareholderforum.com/ACCESS/Library/20130300_Brown-Call-Clement-Sharp.pdf)). Earnings forecasts are where analysts add real value, but only within limits. They beat a random walk for near-term EPS of large, mature firms. That advantage disappears at longer horizons, for younger firms and for loss-makers, and simply extrapolating the one-year-ahead forecast is the most accurate predictor of earnings two and three years out ([Bradshaw, Drake, Myers & Myers](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v17y2012i4d10.1007_s11142-012-9185-8.html)). Analysts also revise sluggishly, which is part of why earnings momentum predicts returns ([Chan, Jegadeesh & Lakonishok](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v51y1996i5p1681-1713.html)).

## Commercial ratings advertise thousands of percent; live records show far less

Commercial products fall into three groups. The first is analyst-judgment ratings: Morningstar's valuation-and-moat stars and CFRA's STARS. The second is single-model products driven by earnings revisions or price trends: Zacks, Value Line and IBD. The third is multi-factor or alternative-data aggregates: Seeking Alpha, TipRanks and MarketBeat. The proposed app belongs to the third group. Across all three, independent evidence repeatedly finds that the signal is real but small after costs, and that it decays.

Zacks is the most transparent about its limits. It reports equal-weighted hypothetical portfolios of Rank #1 stocks, built from the lists clients actually received rather than from a backtest. The returns exclude commissions, spreads and price impact even though the portfolios have been rebalanced weekly since 2014, and Zacks says they are "not achievable with actual portfolios." Accounting firms attested the results through March 2022. The full table also shows bad years, such as #1 stocks falling 40.4% in 2008 against 37.0% for the S&P 500, and 2022, when the #5 bucket beat #3 and #4 ([Zacks Rank disclosure](https://go.zackspro.com/rank-disclosure)). Value Line's Timeliness ranks outperformed size-, value- and momentum-matched benchmarks over 1965–1996, but profitable capture after costs is doubtful ([Choi 2000](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v35y2000i03p485-498_00.html)). The Group 1 minus Group 5 spread has drifted toward zero since the early 1990s and averaged negative in the decade to 2008 ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/investing-expertise/performance-trend-for-value-lines-timeliness-ranking/)). CFRA reports price-only gains of 3,056% for 5-STARS stocks against 663% for the S&P 500 through 2013. Dividends are included only from 2004, and a separate record starts in 2014 when the analyst process changed ([S&P Capital IQ](https://press.spglobal.com/2014-01-14-S-P-Capital-IQ-Equity-Research-Top-Picks-Continue-Outperformance-of-S-P-500-Since-Inception); [CFRA disclosure](https://www.cfraresearch.com/legal/performance-disclosure-stars-performance-charts)).

Morningstar's architecture is closest to the app's planned design. Its fair value comes from a three-stage DCF in which returns on new capital fade to the cost of capital, faster for firms without a moat. Cost of equity comes from a fixed four-bucket table (7.5%, 9%, 11% and 13.5%). An uncertainty rating seeded from trailing 12-month return volatility sets fixed price-to-fair-value bands, and stars are recomputed daily ([Morningstar methodology](https://www.morningstar.com/content/dam/marketing/shared/research/methodology/705988Morningstar_Equity_Research_Methodology.pdf)). Its quantitative arm uses gradient boosting to imitate analyst ratings mechanically. That model explains about 24–35% of the variance in analysts' price-to-fair-value ratios out of sample, applies a ±5% buffer to stop ratings flipping, and caps low-momentum stocks at three stars as a value-trap screen ([Morningstar quantitative methodology](https://s25.q4cdn.com/634000362/files/doc_downloads/1/Quantative-Equity-Research-Effective-2-Dec-2024.pdf)). Independent evidence on whether the stars predict returns is thin. One peer-reviewed event study of 924 five-star upgrades during the 2007–2008 crisis found that upgrades followed price falls and that the stocks then trailed the market by 23.4% over the next 60 days, though they performed as expected on a risk-adjusted basis. The authors concluded the upgrades had little predictive value ([Namjoshi & Hickman](https://jfi-aof.org/index.php/jfi/article/download/2361/1929/7492)).

The quant-driven products publish the least verifiable numbers. Seeking Alpha's Strong Buy record is a backtest from 2010, cited at 4,386% against 528% in one place and 1,754% against 385% in another ([Ryan O'Connell review](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/seeking-alpha-premium-review/); [MoneysMyLife](https://www.moneysmylife.com/seeking-alpha-quant-rating-review/)). Its Sell ratings are explicitly described as a backtested hypothetical portfolio ([Seeking Alpha](https://about.seekingalpha.com/quant-sell-ratings)). TipRanks' Smart Score 10 page showed +461.6% (18.2% a year) in one snapshot and +433.7% (16.9% a year) in another, with no as-of date, weighting or cost disclosure ([TipRanks](https://www.tipranks.com/stocks-to-buy)). Validea splices backtested and live model returns for portfolios launched after 2005 ([Validea user guide](https://zeusnew.validea.com/aboutus/modelportfoliosuserguide.pdf)). AAII once claimed that 91% of its screens beat the S&P 500. After costs, only 32% beat it significantly ([Schadler & Cotten](https://openjournals.libs.uga.edu/fsr/article/view/4917)).

The hardest test is a live, investable, net-of-fee record, and there the gap is large. The IBD 50 ETF, which turns IBD's EPS and relative-strength ratings into a portfolio, returned **1.88% a year from April 2015 to December 2024 against 13.29% for the S&P 500** ([Innovator FFTY](https://innovatoretfs.com/fund/ffty)). Across 238 US smart-beta ETFs, market-adjusted returns fell from about 3% a year in pre-listing backtests to **between −0.5% and −1% after listing**, which the authors attribute to data mining in index construction ([Huang, Song & Xiang 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v59y2024i6p2515-2546_2.html)). Multi-factor indexes, where sponsors freely choose and combine factors, fell from 4.11% to −0.79% ([HKU press release](https://hkubs.hku.hk/media/press-release/disrupt-your-traditional-perception-to-etfs-discover-how-short-interest-of-etfs-benefits-the-stock-market)). Vanguard found that 87% of 370 new indexes beat the market in their backfilled history and only 51% did once live ([Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/b14zpnk4mmzk55/study-finds-many-etf-indexes-misleading)). A multi-factor composite score, the category the app belongs to, sits where data-mining risk is highest.

Regulators have codified the difference between credible and misleading track records. GIPS bars hypothetical or backtested results from performance composites and allows them only as clearly labeled supplemental information ([GIPS Q&A](https://www.gipsstandards.org/?GIPSID=330)). The SEC's Marketing Rule requires net results alongside gross ones and sets conditions for hypothetical performance ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2020-334)). In 2023 nine advisers paid $850,000 combined for showing hypothetical performance on public websites, and the SEC said such performance would generally be inappropriate for a mass audience ([Sidley](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2023/09/sec-marketing-rule-enforcement-charges-against-nine-registered-investment-advisers)). FINRA Rule 2241 requires broker research to define each rating's horizon and benchmark, show the firm-wide Buy/Hold/Sell distribution and chart every rating and target change, and give each target a reasonable basis, a stated valuation method and its risks ([FINRA 2241](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/2241)). The amendment to FINRA Rule 2210 approved on 15 October 2026 permits performance projections only for audiences able to evaluate them, not mass retail ([Alston & Bird](https://www.alston.com/en/insights/publications/2026/10/sec-approves-finra-rule-2210-change)).

## The app should promise calibration and transparency, not alpha

The evidence sets a realistic goal for the product. Even an excellent model will be right on direction only slightly more often than a coin flip for individual stocks. It can still beat the sell-side on calibration, consistency and candor, which are the areas where bank research is documented to fail. Each design choice below follows from that goal.

### Ratings: relative, balanced, sticky and always paired with a target

Banks define ratings relative to a benchmark, not as absolute return calls. Morgan Stanley judges Overweight, Equal-weight and Underweight against the analyst's industry coverage universe over 12–18 months ([Morgan Stanley disclosures](https://www.morganstanley.com/eqr/disclosures/webapp/generalresearch)). Goldman launched its relative three-tier system in 2002 with 26% Outperform, 52% In-Line and 22% Underperform ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pressroom/press-releases/2002/2002-11-04)). The app should define Buy, Hold and Sell in every report as expected 12-month total return relative to the stock's sector ETF, with SPY as a secondary benchmark, as Rule 2241 requires of broker research. A relative definition forces a real share of Sells, and the evidence that upgrades from stingy brokers are more informative is an argument for keeping the Buy share well below the street's 56%. To limit rating churn, the app should copy Novy-Marx and Velikov's buy/hold spread and Morningstar's ±5% buffer: require a bigger score move to change a rating than to keep it. Every report should carry a target, including Holds and Sells, which fixes the sell-side habit of dropping targets on bad news and makes the record complete. Since the level of a rating has shown little net predictive value among the largest firms, the report should present the rating as a summary of the thesis, not as the product. Report structure can mirror Rule 2241: rating and range on the cover, then the thesis, an estimates table, the valuation method and its inputs, catalysts, and risks to the target, followed by an appendix with rating definitions, the app's own distribution across all 500 names and a rating-history chart for each stock. Professional clients value industry knowledge and management access most, and the app cannot provide management access, so its advantage has to come from transparency, with every number traceable to a cited filing.

### Price ranges: roll fair value forward and close the gap slowly

Morningstar's fair value has no fixed horizon and grows each year at the cost of equity minus the dividend yield ([Morningstar methodology](https://www.morningstar.com/content/dam/marketing/shared/research/methodology/705988Morningstar_Equity_Research_Methodology.pdf)). The gap between value and price closes over years, earning only 5.3% of a 27.8% three-year spread in the first 12 months. So the base case should be today's price grown at the cost of equity minus the dividend yield, plus a partial closing of the value gap, for example one-third a year to match three-year convergence. It should not be a fair value that the stock is assumed to reach in twelve months. A base case implying large upside on an S&P 500 name should be treated as low-probability by construction, since the hit rate of sell-side targets falls steeply as implied upside rises.

The valuation engine itself should follow the accuracy evidence. Peer multiples should use same-industry harmonic means, favor equity-value P/E over EV/sales, and show the multiple and the EPS separately so each can be graded. When near-term earnings are negative, the engine should drop undefined multiples rather than impute them. The DCF should cap terminal growth at the risk-free rate, set reinvestment from growth and return on capital (or, for high-growth firms, from a sector sales-to-capital ratio), fade margins toward mature peers and never close with an exit multiple ([Damodaran](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/papers/younggrowth.pdf)). A residual-income cross-check anchored on book value reduces dependence on the terminal value. Banks and insurers need equity-level models: excess returns on book value, with price-to-book explained by ROE. Among US banks with market caps above $1 billion in February 2009, the correlation between P/B and ROE exceeded 0.70 ([Damodaran, financial firms](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/papers/finfirm09.pdf)). Each report should state the terminal value's share of total value, and a high share should raise the uncertainty level.

Range width should reflect how much of the outcome is unpredictable. As an illustration, a stock with 30% annual volatility and a 60% firm-specific variance share carries about ±23% of one-standard-deviation idiosyncratic risk on its own, before adding discount-rate scenarios that can move value by many points. If the bear and bull cases are labeled as, say, a 10th–90th percentile range, about 80% of realized prices should land inside. That coverage test is one the sell-side literature never reports, and the app can win on it. Morningstar's documented price-to-fair-value bands offer a ready baseline for scaling ratings and ranges to uncertainty:

| Uncertainty | 5-star at price/FV ≤ | 3-star band | 1-star at price/FV ≥ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 80% | 95–105% | 125% |
| Medium | 70% | 90–110% | 135% |
| High | 60% | 85–115% | 155% |
| Very high | 50% | 80–125% | 175% |
| Extreme | 25% | 50–200% | 400% |

### Composite score: rank within sectors and weight toward what survives

The six-pillar score should be rebuilt around the evidence rather than weighted equally by default. Profitability and quality deserve the highest weight. Momentum deserves substantial weight, should be scaled down when the market has recently fallen and volatility is high, and should be disclosed as price-based rather than filings-based. Valuation should use several sector-relative metrics (earnings, cash flow, sales) and be read together with profitability, not as a standalone cheapness call. Growth should get near-zero weight or be redefined as capital discipline, with low net issuance and low investment scoring well, since issuance is among the most robust low-turnover signals. Balance-sheet strength and accruals should act as safety screens and low-weight inputs. Restatements, material weaknesses and going-concern opinions should cap ratings as overrides rather than enter the ranking. Scoring should be integrated at the stock level, as percentiles within GICS sectors, with weights fixed and published before launch. The team should also disclose how many weighting variants it tried, because composite indexes whose sponsors freely choose factors show the largest gap between backtest and live performance. A realistic expectation for the Buy-minus-Sell spread within sectors is tens of basis points a month gross at most, in line with Chen and Velikov's estimate of about 20 basis points net for combined anomalies.

### Track record: grade live calls against base rates, for years

The track record is where the app can be more credible than both banks and vendors. Only live, time-stamped (ideally hashed) calls should count. Any backtest should be labeled supplemental and never spliced onto the live record. Each methodology version should keep its own record, as CFRA does. Results should cover all three buckets, equal- and cap-weighted, gross and after a stated cost assumption, as annualized excess returns with confidence intervals and calendar-year tables rather than cumulative percentages. Because a winning factor tilt can look like skill, the app should report factor-adjusted alpha alongside raw sector-relative returns. EPS forecasts should be graded against figures frozen on the report date. The benchmarks below give each metric an external reference point:

| Metric | External reference | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Price at or beyond base target at month 12 | ~38% for sell-side, 2000–2009 | [Bradshaw, Brown & Huang](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v18y2013i4d10.1007_s11142-012-9216-5.html) |
| Target touched at any time within 12 months | ~64% (2000–2009); 54% (1997–1999) | [BBH](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v18y2013i4d10.1007_s11142-012-9216-5.html); [Asquith et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9246.pdf) |
| Signed error (implied minus realized return) | +15 points sell-side optimism | [BBH](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v18y2013i4d10.1007_s11142-012-9216-5.html) |
| Mean absolute target error | ~45% | [BBH](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v18y2013i4d10.1007_s11142-012-9216-5.html) |
| Share of outcomes inside the bear–bull range | No published benchmark; should match the range's stated probability | Calibration test |
| Directional hit rate vs sector | ~55–57% ceiling implied by best large-cap annual R² | [Gu, Kelly & Xiu](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf) |
| Buy-minus-Sell spread | Combined anomalies net ~20 bps/month; average single anomaly ~4 | [Chen & Velikov](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/zeroing-in-on-the-expected-returns-of-anomalies.htm) |
| Buy list vs S&P 500 | 21% of large-cap funds beat the index in 2025, net | [SPIVA](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf) |
| Rating distribution | Street S&P 500: 56.4% Buy / 38.7% Hold / 4.9% Sell | [FactSet](https://advantage.factset.com/hubfs/Website/Resources%20Section/Research%20Desk/Earnings%20Insight/EarningsInsight_062725C.pdf) |

Statistical power is the hardest constraint. A binomial approximation shows that telling a 57% hit rate from 50% at conventional confidence takes roughly 200 independent calls, and telling 55% from 50% takes nearly 400. Calls on S&P 500 stocks in the same year share market and sector shocks, so 500 annual ratings are worth far fewer independent observations. Several years of record will be needed before a claim of skill is defensible, and the product copy should say so from launch. Momentum crashes add a known risk. In sharp rebounds after bear markets, Sells on past losers will look worst, and the app should warn of that in advance. Legal exposure also needs checking. The SEC's position on hypothetical performance for mass audiences and FINRA's exclusion of retail audiences from projections both bear on a public site that publishes price ranges and backtests. Whether the app falls under the Investment Advisers Act's publisher exclusion was not established in this research and is a question for securities counsel.

## Conclusion

The research changes what "accuracy" should mean for this product. No traditional method lets an S&P 500 rating engine out-predict the market on individual 12-month calls. But the sell-side's measurable failures (15 points of optimism, targets dropped on bad news, a Buy share near 56%, conflicted incentives) are design choices, not facts about markets. An independent engine can avoid all of them. The app's headline metrics should therefore be calibration and honesty: range coverage that matches its stated probability, signed target error near zero, a balanced distribution, and no missing targets. The Buy-minus-Sell spread should be a secondary, long-run statistic that may take years to separate from noise. There is also a tension between the two goals. The more the composite leans on what works (profitability and momentum), the more its Buys will look like known factor exposures, so reporting factor-adjusted alongside raw returns is what lets users tell skill from style.

The evidence also leaves a gap the app can fill. None of the reviewed sources reports out-of-sample predictability for S&P 500 members graded against sectors, which is exactly the app's setting. Before launch, the team can estimate it from the free Jensen–Kelly–Pedersen and Chen–Zimmermann factor data restricted to the largest 500 stocks, publish the result as labeled supplemental context, and fix the methodology. After launch, a decade of time-stamped, fully disclosed, sector-relative ratings would be a public record that neither the academic literature nor any commercial vendor currently provides.
